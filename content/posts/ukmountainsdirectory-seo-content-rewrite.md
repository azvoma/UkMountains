## How many Munros are in England?

Short answer: **none.** A Munro is, by definition, a Scottish mountain over 3,000ft (914m) named after Sir Hugh Munro, who published the first list in 1891 — the classification simply doesn't apply south of the border.

England has its own equivalent system instead:

- **Hewitts** — "Hills in England, Wales and Ireland over Two Thousand feet," with at least 30m of prominence.
- **Nuttalls** — English and Welsh summits over 2,000ft with 15m of prominence.
- **Furths** — British Isles summits over 3,000ft *furth of* (outside) Scotland, effectively England, Wales and Ireland's answer to a Munro.

Scafell Pike, England's highest peak at 978m, is classed as both a Hewitt and a Furth.

## Frequently asked questions {#faqs}

**Are there any mountains in Kent or London?**
No. By the roughly 600m (2,000 ft) convention used across the UK, neither Kent nor Greater London has any mountains — only hills. Kent's highest point, Betsom's Hill near Westerham, tops out at around 251m; London's highest natural ground sits at a similar height on its southern fringe. Both are proper hills, just not mountains.

**What's the difference between a hill and a mountain name list?**
A "hill" list has no fixed lower bound and includes everything from gentle rises to near-mountain summits. A "mountain" list is generally restricted to peaks above roughly 600m, though the exact cutoff varies by source and classification system.

**What are the mountain ranges near me?**
It depends entirely on your location — someone in the Scottish Highlands has dozens of Munros on their doorstep, while most of southern England has none. Use the directory above to find your nearest range by country.

**Is there a difference between "hewitt mountain" and "Munro"?**
Yes. Munros apply only in Scotland; Hewitts ("Hills in England, Wales and Ireland over Two Thousand feet") are the equivalent classification used south of the border.

## Key Ben Nevis facts

- **Location:** Lochaber, Highland, Scotland — overlooking Fort William and Glen Nevis.
- **First ascent:** 19 August 1771, by James Robertson.
- **Classification:** Munro, Marilyn, and the UK's country high point.
- **North face:** cliffs drop around 600m into Coire Leis, among the most significant in Britain for winter climbing.
- **South and west flanks:** rise roughly 1,200m in just 2km above the River Nevis — the longest continuous hill slope in Britain.

When cross-checking route reports for this page, one edge case worth flagging: the summit plateau can hold snow well into summer, and disorientation near the cliff edges in poor visibility is a genuine, well-documented hazard — not just a guidebook cliché.

## Other summits on the Cadair Idris ridge

Cadair Idris isn't a single peak but a ridge system with several named tops — a detail worth knowing before you plan a route, since "the summit" can mean different things depending on which one you're heading for.

- **Penygadair** — 893m, the true summit.
- **Mynydd Moel** — 863m, the second-highest point on the ridge.
- **Cyfrwy** — 811m, on the western end.
- **Craig Cwm Amarch** — 791m.

**Common mistake to avoid:** planning a walk to "Cadair Idris" without checking which summit — the classic horseshoe route (Penygadair, Mynydd Moel and Craig Cwm Amarch) is a significantly longer day than a straightforward Pony Path ascent to Penygadair alone.

## The legend of Cadair Idris

The name translates as "Chair of Idris," after a giant said to have used the mountain as his seat. The most enduring legend claims that anyone who spends a night alone on its slopes will wake either a poet or a madman — a story still repeated by local guides today, and one that gives the mountain a mythic reputation matched by few other Welsh peaks.
